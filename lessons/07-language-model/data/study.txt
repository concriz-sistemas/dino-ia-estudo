does anyone teach the ai the rule?
no. in no lesson does the ai receive the rule "jump when the cactus is close". in lesson 1, the rule exists in the code only to generate 600 already-answered examples, and the network learns from the examples without ever seeing the rule. in lessons 3 and 4 not even that: the ai only gets a score or a reward, which says whether it worked, never how to do it.
does the dino learn by itself to see where the obstacles are?
no, and that is on purpose. in no lesson does the dino see the screen. the game code measures everything and hands over 6 ready-made numbers: the distance, width, height and elevation of the next obstacle, the dino's height and the speed. it is like giving it a distance sensor instead of eyes. what it learns by itself is what to do with those numbers: when to jump, when to duck, when to wait. learning to see from the screen's pixels, as deepmind did with atari games, requires a convolutional network and much more training. that is lesson 6: there, a cnn learns to estimate those 6 numbers from the screen, and the dino plays from pixels alone.
what about lesson 1, then?
lesson 1 has no dino playing at all. it is an exercise with ready-made examples (the answer key, the dots in the charts) to watch the gradient work up close. the ones that really learn to play, with nobody knowing the answer, are the dinos of lessons 3 and 4.
so is the learning random?
only partly. the weights start out random in every lesson. in lessons 1 and 4, from then on the gradient computes which way to move each weight, with no dice involved. in lesson 3, the mutations are random, but the selection is not: the dinos that get further have children. chance proposes and the score decides. that is why each seed gives different numbers, but the main rule ("far = don't jump") always shows up.
why does the shape in the replay appear quickly and then barely change?
at first the network is very wrong, the gradient is large and each step moves the weights a lot. near the bottom of the loss "valley", the gradient becomes tiny (from 1 to 0.002 in lesson 1) and the weights almost stop. this is called convergence. lesson 1 has the full explanation, including the link to linear regression.
why does lesson 4's training stall and then jump?
for about 600 thousand steps, the dino is stuck on "jump nonstop", which earns about 150 points and is hard to give up (a local optimum). when it discovers that waiting for the cactus to come close pays much more, the average shoots up to almost 5000. in the first tests, without tweaks, it did not leave the plateau within 500 thousand steps, and with 2 million it only left near the end. what brought the jump forward was making each action last 2 frames and giving a bigger bonus for exploring.
does the ai do what a human would?
not always. facing a bird at head height, lesson 4's dino stays ducked while the bird is far away and jumps over it when it gets close. nobody taught it that, and it works. the reward pays for the result, not for the method.
why does the dino play better in testing than in training?
during training, lesson 4's agent still picks random actions to explore, so sometimes it makes mistakes on purpose. in testing it always picks the most likely action. that is why the training average stays around 4,300, while the final model scored 5000 in all 10 new matches.
once trained, is the learning stored somewhere?
yes, in a weights file. lesson 4's dino is the file lessons/04-reinforcement/results/models/ppo02000k.zip: about 62 kb holding 2,692 numbers. loading that file means having the dino that knows how to play, without training again. in lesson 1, the last row of weighthistory.csv has the final weights; in lesson 3, champions.pkl stores the champion of each generation.
is this similar to an llm?
it is the same idea at a gigantic scale. an llm is also a weights file, only with billions of numbers and anywhere from a few to hundreds of gigabytes. and, as here, using it does not change the file: running watch.py a thousand times teaches the dino nothing, and an llm does not learn while it talks with you. it only changes when a new training run produces a new file, that is, a new version of the model.
they use the same building blocks as these lessons, at a huge scale:
pre-training - the cycle of lesson 1 (forward, loss, gradient, step), with billions of weights learning to predict the next word in trillions of texts. in a way it is already imitation: the model copies how people write.
supervised fine-tuning - lesson 5: people write examples of good answers and the model learns to imitate them.
reinforcement learning - the cycle of lesson 4 (act, get a reward, adjust by gradient): the model generates answers, gets a score (from people or from automatic tests) and is adjusted to score better.
the neuroevolution of lesson 3 is not used to train llms (it would be far too slow with billions of weights), but it is the most visual way to understand that "learning" means adjusting numbers until a score improves.

before putting an ai in the game, let's solve one piece of it on its own:
the right answer is a band: if the cactus is too close, it's already too late; if it's too far, you land before reaching it. and the faster the game, the earlier you need to jump.
the network never sees this rule. it only gets 600 examples (the dots above) and has to discover the pattern on its own. the terminal confirms it: "600 examples, 137 of them are 'jump'."
they are the answer key. before training, the script draws 600 random situations and uses the true rule to answer each one. from then on, in the network's charts, the black dots are the 137 examples marked as "jump" (the 463 "don't jump" ones are left out so the chart stays clean). the legend calls them "answer key: 'jump' examples".
the dots never change. what changes during training is the colored background: the network's answer for each spot on the plane. training means making the background turn red where the dots are and blue everywhere else.
this is called supervised learning: the answers to the examples are known from the start, and the network learns by comparing what it answered with the answer key. in lessons 3 and 4 there is no answer key; the dino only gets a score or a reward.
every neuron always does the same thing:
the sum inside the activation, weightinput + weightinput + bias, is exactly the formula of a linear regression. section 6 shows what that means in practice.
this network has 2x6 + 6 + 6x1 + 1 = 25 adjustable numbers. training = finding good values for these 25 numbers. (a large llm has hundreds of billions of them - but the idea is the same.)
to improve, the network needs a score. the loss is a number that gets big when the network is wrong and small when it is right. we use binary cross-entropy, which punishes hard whoever is wrong with confidence (saying 99% "jump" when the answer was "don't jump").
picture the loss as hilly terrain, where each position is a combination of the 25 weights. we want to reach the valley.
the gradient answers, for each weight: "if i increase this weight a tiny bit, does the loss go up or down? and by how much?"
positive gradient  increasing the weight makes it worse  so we decrease it.
negative gradient  increasing the weight makes it better  so we increase it.

the backward() function applies the chain rule from calculus, from back to front: first the error at the output, then how much each hidden neuron contributed to it, then how much each input weight contributed to the neuron. that's why it's called backpropagation: the error flows back through the network.
the script compares the formula with a brute-force estimate (it nudges the weight by +0.00001 and -0.00001 and measures the difference in the loss). if they match, backpropagation is correct. in the terminal this appears under "checking the gradient  formula  vs  brute force":

the accuracy goes up from 46% (guessing) to 100%.
the gradient keeps shrinking: near the valley, the terrain gets flat and the steps get small.

in the replay (--live) you can see that the band shows up in the first few hundred epochs and then barely changes. there are two phases:
fast descent (epochs 0 to 300). with random weights, the network is very wrong, so the gradient is big. since the step is rate x gradient, the steps are big too, and the shape appears. the loss drops sharply.
fine-tuning (from 300 on). the network is already close to the bottom of the loss valley. the terrain gets flat, the gradient gets small (watch the "total size" in the gradient panel drop from 1 at epoch 0 to 0.03 at 300 and 0.002 at 4000) and each step moves the weights very little. this is called convergence.
the shape doesn't stand completely still: the weights keep growing slowly (weights panel), and that makes the edges of the band sharper. the network is getting more confident. with cross-entropy, you can always lower the loss a little more by betting with more certainty, so the weights grow without stopping, more and more slowly.
section 8 shows these two phases from above, on a map of the loss.
every neuron computes wdistance + wspeed + b, the formula of a linear regression. where that sum is zero, the neuron flips from "no" to "yes", and those points form a straight line on the chart. the activation (tanh or sigmoid) only bends the answer near the line; it doesn't change where the line is.
a neuron with a sigmoid and no hidden layer is a logistic regression: it can only draw one straight line. the band needs two edges, so it gives up and learns "never jump" - which gets 77% right only because most examples are "don't jump". this is a classic trap: high accuracy doesn't mean it learned.
each purple line is where one hidden neuron flips. the thicker the line, the more that neuron matters to the output.
epoch 0: all six lines cross in the middle of the chart. the biases start at zero, so every line passes through the center point; only the angles are random.
epoch 50 and epoch 300: the lines spread out and rotate toward the dots.
epoch 4000: two neurons sit exactly on the two edges of the band, with strong output weights of opposite signs (+9.7 and -9.4): one says "the cactus is close enough", the other says "but not too close". the other four moved their lines out of the playing area (to distances above 2) and barely matter anymore.
so the network only needed 2 of its 6 neurons, which matches what nhidden = 2 shows in the exercises. the output layer combines the two lines into the band. more neurons = more lines to combine = more complicated shapes.
you can watch these lines move in the replay (--live).
they are at the top of the script. they are not learned - you choose them.

three things to notice:
the valley is a long, narrow bowl. going across it is steep, along it is gentle. a big step overshoots across the steep side and bounces; that is the zigzag. bigger networks can have valleys like this in many dimensions at once.
the two phases of section 5 show up here too. the fast descent is the first few steps, when the paths drop toward the valley; the fine-tuning is the long, slow crawl along the bottom.
the cosine schedule gets the best of both: big steps at the start, when the network is far from the bottom, and small steps at the end, when it is close. the learning rate follows half a cosine wave, from 0.78 down to 0 (bottom-right chart). until about step 50 the purple path has a lower loss than the green one, and it ends practically tied with it. big models, including llms, are usually trained with a schedule like this instead of a fixed rate.
a neuron with an activation (like the ones in trainfromscratch.py) has a curvier valley than this perfect bowl, but the walk is the same: weight = weight - learningrate  gradient, over and over.

an llm is trained with exactly this cycle - forward, loss, gradient, step - except that:
the input is a piece of text and the output is the probability of each next word;
there are hundreds of billions of weights instead of 25;
the data is trillions of words instead of 600 dots;
they use smarter variations of gradient descent (like adam), on thousands of gpus.
a vector is just a list of numbers. each hidden neuron in this lesson has one: its two input weights, (weight of the distance, weight of the speed). that vector sets the direction of the neuron's line.
cosine similarity measures how much two vectors point the same way, ignoring their size: 1 = same direction, 0 = unrelated, -1 = opposite directions.
in the trained network:
the two neurons on the edges of the band have vectors (-8.91, 3.07) and (-8.15, 1.97). their cosine similarity is 0.996: they point almost the same way, which is why the two edges of the band are almost parallel.
another neuron, with tiny weights (-0.17, 0.08), also scores 0.995 against the first one. it points the same way but is 50 times weaker. cosine similarity only looks at direction, not size.
llms use the same tool on a much bigger scale. they turn each word or text into a vector with hundreds or thousands of numbers (an embedding). texts with similar meaning end up pointing in similar directions, so "dog" and "puppy" have a high cosine similarity and "dog" and "spreadsheet" a low one. that is how search engines and assistants find text with a similar meaning, even when the words are different.

in lesson 1, each weight was adjusted by calculating which direction made the loss go down. here there is no calculation at all:
nobody programmed "jump when the cactus is close". that rule emerges because, by chance, some dinos jump at the right moment, survive and pass their weights on.
neat (neuroevolution of augmenting topologies, 2002) is the algorithm used here, through the neat-python library. it has three tricks:
starts minimal: only inputs connected to outputs, no hidden layer. neurons and connections are added by mutation when they help.
genes with history: each connection has an "innovation" number, so two parents can cross networks of different shapes without scrambling everything.
species: similar networks compete among themselves. a freshly created innovation (which at first usually makes the network worse) gets time to mature without being wiped out by the established champions.
train.py has a recorder that, at the end of every generation, saves to results/history.json:
the best score, average, median and standard deviation of the population;
the species and their sizes;
the full champion: every neuron with its bias and every connection with its weight;
all the weights of the whole population.
history.json is plain text. open it in an editor and search for "champion" to see a whole network in numbers.
report.py turns this into charts and into report.md:
neat draws random mutations, but train.py fixes the starting point of that randomness with seed = 0: it seeds python's random before creating the population. so your run gives exactly the same numbers as the example run in results/report.md (30 generations, 50 dinos per generation). the values in parentheses come from it. if you change seed, you get a different evolution with other numbers, but the patterns repeat:
the best climbs in jumps, the average climbs slowly. one lucky dino reaches 5000 early (generation 6 in the example), but the whole population takes much longer to improve (the average goes from 121 in generation 0 to 1084 in the last one).
the best of a generation can be worse than the previous one's (669  316  235 in generations 0, 1 and 2 of the example; and after reaching 5000 in generation 6, generation 7 dropped to 1477, then 5000 in every generation from 8 to 29). each generation plays a different match, with another sequence of obstacles, and sometimes the match is harder. this is on purpose: if they all played the same level, the dinos would memorize the level instead of learning to play (overfitting).
the median stays low even while the average rises. most children get bad mutations and die early. evolution is wasteful: many try, few succeed.
the network's size varies from run to run. sometimes the mutations cut connections and the champion ends up simpler than it started, with 18 connections (6 connections and 1 hidden neuron in the example); sometimes it gains hidden neurons. see 03complexity.png and 06networks.png. neat does not look for the "prettiest" network, just one that works.
the main rule always shows up: obstacle far = don't jump. in the example it comes directly, as a connection distance  jump with weight -5.204, the strongest of the final champion. with other seeds it can go through a hidden neuron instead: distance  hidden neuron with a positive weight, and hidden neuron  jump with a negative weight. positive times negative is negative, that is, the same rule by another path. nobody taught the dinos this.
the other strong weights of the example's final champion: speed  duck (-1.921), speed  nothing (-0.993), hidden 178  jump (+0.632) and elevation  hidden 178 (+0.585). the full table is in section 6 of the report.

reinforcement sits in the middle: as in lesson 3, nobody says which move is right; as in lesson 1, the weights change by gradient, with no random mutations.
environment.py wraps the game in the standard gymnasium format, which any reinforcement learning algorithm understands:
observation: the same 6 sensors from the previous lessons.
action: 0 = nothing, 1 = jump, 2 = duck.
reward: +0.1 per frame alive and -1 on crashing. it is the only "teacher". the agent does not know what a cactus is; it only knows that some numbers in the input come before the bad reward.
every match has freshly drawn obstacles, so the agent has to generalize instead of memorizing a level.
action repeat: each decision lasts 2 game frames (actionrepeat = 2). with fewer decisions per match, it is easier to link an action to its consequence. deepmind did the same with 4 frames in atari games.
ppo trains two small networks at the same time, both with 6 inputs and two layers of 32 neurons:
actor (1,379 weights): looks at the sensors and gives the chance of each action. it is the one that plays.
critic (1,313 weights): looks at the sensors and estimates the value: how much reward is still to come from there on.
in watch.py, the trained model's value ("expected value:" on the panel) hovers around 19.5. that is no accident: each step is worth 0.2 (two frames of 0.1) and the discount gamma = 0.99 makes the distant future worth less and less, so the sum of everything ahead comes to 0.2 / (1 - 0.99) = 20. that is the ceiling, and the value shown stays just under it. when a hard obstacle approaches and the critic thinks things may go wrong, the value drops.
one cycle, repeated about 245 times in a 2-million-step training run:
play: 8 matches in parallel, 1,024 steps each. the actor draws its actions from the chances it gives (that is how it explores).
compare with what was expected: for each step, compute the advantage: did it give more or less reward than the critic expected?
adjust by gradient: increase the chance of actions with positive advantage and decrease that of actions with negative advantage. the critic is also adjusted to be less wrong next time.
brake: the clip (the "p" in proximal) keeps the chance of any action from changing more than 20% in one update. without this brake, one batch of lucky matches could ruin a policy that already worked.
step 3 is exactly the gradient descent from lesson 1. the difference is what we try to reduce: there it was the error against the right answer; here it is "less reward than there could have been".
the example training run is in results/report.md. every training run gives slightly different numbers, but the patterns repeat.
plateau, then a jump. for about 600 thousand steps the agent stays around 100-250 points: jumping nonstop is a "good enough" strategy that is hard to let go of (a local optimum). suddenly it discovers that waiting for the cactus to get close pays off much more, and the average shoots up (the moving average first reaches 300 at 595k steps, 1,000 at 686k and 4,000 at 867k).
training vs. test. during training the score stays around 4,200-4,900 (the last 200 matches average 4,329), below the maximum, because the actor still draws actions to explore. in the tests, report.py and watch.py always pick the most likely action.
each saved model on 10 new matches. the models at 0k, 250k and 500k steps average 86, 116 and 145 points. the first saved model with a mean of 4,000 or more is the 750k one, and from 750k on every model scored 5000 in all 10 new matches (5000 is scorelimit, where the match ends).
entropy plunges together with the jump in scores: the agent stops choosing at random and becomes decisive.
top: the network learned a slanted boundary (jump when the cactus gets close, and earlier the faster the game), similar to the one in lesson 1. except that here nobody gave it examples.
bottom: with the bird at head height, the agent stays ducked while it is far away and jumps over it when it gets close. it is not what a human would do, but it works. the reward says whether it worked, never how to do it.
in a weights file. once trained, the model is just that: numbers. loading the file = having the dino that knows how to play.
an llm is the same thing on a giant scale: a file (usually .safetensors) with billions of weights, taking from a few to hundreds of gigabytes. the network's structure is different (a transformer), but the idea is the same: training adjusts the numbers, and then the file is loaded to be used.
and, as here, using it does not change the file. running watch.py a thousand times teaches the dino nothing; only a new train.py run changes the weights. an llm does not learn while it talks to you either: what it "learns" comes from new rounds of training, which produce a new weights file, that is, a new version of the model.

llms are trained in the same order as this study:
pre-training is imitation on a huge scale: the model learns to predict the next word of trillions of words written by people. it copies human text, good and bad.
supervised fine-tuning is this lesson: people write examples of good answers, and the model learns to imitate them. it's cheap and fast, like the 1-match student, but the model can only be as good as the examples, like the weak-teacher student.
reinforcement learning (lesson 4) comes last: the model tries, gets a score, and is adjusted to score higher. that is how it can go beyond its examples.
imitating first and then using reinforcement combines the strengths of both: imitation gives a good starting point cheaply, and reinforcement improves it where the examples fall short.

the screen is 800 x 300 pixels, but most of it doesn't matter. vision.py crops the area from the top of a jump to just below the ground, and up to 600 pixels in front of the dino, shrinks every 3 x 5 block of pixels into one and turns it to grayscale. the network gets a 200 x 44 image, with the dark things (dino, cacti, birds) turned bright.
one image is not enough. in a photo you can't tell how fast something moves, and the speed decides when to jump. so the network gets two images: the current one and the one from 3 frames ago. the difference between them is the movement.
the top row shows what the network receives: the scene now, and "now minus 3 frames ago", where the cactus appears twice (red where it is now, blue where it was).
a convolution slides a small filter (here 5 x 5 pixels) over the whole image and, at each position, measures how much that piece of the image looks like the filter. the same filter is used everywhere, so a cactus on the left and a cactus on the right are detected the same way. that is what makes cnns good at images.
the eyes have 3 convolutional layers and then a dense layer that turns what they found into the 6 sensor numbers: 630,406 weights in total.
layer 1 (the second row of the image above) reacts to small details: the outline of the dino and of the cactus.
layer 2 combines them into small shapes.
layer 3 sees much less detail but bigger things: one blob for the dino, one for the cactus. it says "something is here".
these are the 16 filters of the first layer, as learned:
in textbooks, first-layer filters look like neat edge detectors. these look noisier: the images are tiny and the training is short. some still show horizontal or vertical stripes of bright and dark.
one extra trick: convolutions are good at saying what is in the image but not where. the eyes get an extra input channel that just says "how far to the right is this pixel" (a technique called coordconv), which helps them measure the distance.
end to end (pixelstokeys.py): one network, from the two images straight to "nothing / jump / duck", copying lesson 4's agent, like the student of lesson 5.
eyes + brain (traineyes.py): the cnn only estimates the 6 sensor numbers (the eyes). lesson 4's agent, unchanged, decides from those numbers (the brain). the brain doesn't know whether the numbers came from the game or from the eyes.
without any extra trick, the end-to-end network did better than eyes + brain. but when the end-to-end network crashes, there's no way to know why: it is one block from pixels to keys. with eyes + brain you can compare, scene by scene, what the eyes estimated with the truth. that is what showed the problem, and what led to the fix.
in round 0 the eyes learn only from scenes recorded while the brain plays with the true sensors. their average error near obstacles is 55 pixels, and eyes + brain crash early in every match (mean 316).
following one crash frame by frame, in an earlier version of these eyes, showed the cause. while the cactus is far, lesson 4's agent likes to duck. when the cactus gets close, it is normally already jumping, so the recorded scenes almost never show a ducking dino right next to a cactus. with the eyes in charge, the jump comes a tiny bit late, the dino is still ducking in that position, and the eyes, facing a scene they have never seen, go wrong: the estimated distance grows while the cactus comes closer. the brain thinks the cactus is far away and doesn't jump.
it's the trap from lesson 5 again, now in perception: a network trained only on the teacher's scenes doesn't know what to do in the scenes its own mistakes lead to.
dagger (dataset aggregation) closes that gap:
eyes + brain play together, mistakes and all.
every scene they get into is recorded with the true sensor numbers as the answer key (the game always knows them).
the eyes keep training on everything recorded so far.
repeat.
each round adds exactly the scenes the system was getting wrong.
the first rounds add few scenes, because eyes + brain still crash early. as the eyes get better, the matches get longer, each round brings more scenes, and the remaining mistakes go away.
after round 6, the distance the eyes estimate (orange) follows the true one (black) for every obstacle in a match.
to train "cat or dog" on photos, someone has to label thousands of pictures by hand. here, every image comes with its labels for free: the game knows the exact distance, size and height of every obstacle. that is also why dagger was easy: any new scene could be labeled at once.
self-driving cars and robots use the same idea. a lot of their training happens in simulators, where every pixel comes with its answer. the hard part is the same as here: the real world shows them scenes the simulator never did.

models that see images work in a similar way: a vision network turns the image into lists of numbers, and the language model reads those numbers as if they were words. that's eyes + brain again, with a much bigger brain.
scenes never seen in training are a problem for llms too. when a question is very different from anything in its training data, a model can answer with confidence and still be wrong, just like the eyes that "saw" a cactus far away when it was right in front of the dino.
labels are the expensive part. here the game gave them for free. for llms, good examples written by people are costly, which is one reason they are pre-trained on raw text first: the next word is a label that comes for free.
